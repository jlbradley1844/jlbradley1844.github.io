## America's Anti-Meritocratic Moment
I've been catching up on some old shows, in this case watching _Breaking Bad_. Even though
the show is incredibly good, I'm reminded of a tweet (I refuse to call it anything else) that
I read last year. It went something like this:

> With all these morally complex dramas on streaming TV, I'm afraid we need to reintroduce
> the Greek Chorus. "This person is bad!" "This is not good!!" "Pride goes before the downfall."
> So many Americans insist on learning the wrong lessons from these TV shows.

So you have Bryan Cranston, formerly typecast as "America's clueless Dad," confronted with
one moral choice after another, and every time he makes the _worst_ decision. But rather than
watch in horror, your typical American empathizes with this antihero, and regards his wife on
the show, who is simply complicit, with horror and disgust. Menwhile, Walter White is regarded
as some sort of bad@ass father figure, in spite of the fact that "I'm doing all of this only to protect my
family" is (spoiler alert) a big lie he keeps telling himself so he can live with his decisions.

In a coda that will surprise no one, the tweet was written by the show's creator, Vince Gilligan.
Indeed he said in a recent interview
that he hopes Hollywood will lavish more attention on "good guys," now that bad guys seem
to be running everything.

Walter White has decided he wants to WIN, and apparently that what we want our Real Men to do.

It is said that we live in a meritocracy. Many are skeptical of this claim. But consider these
facts about modern life in the U.S.:

* It is assumed that the hardest-working, most adept workers, are the ones that get the promotion, or at least _should_;
* We demand competence in our highest-paying professions, and use credentials and important degrees to ensure capability to do the job;
* It is assumed that the best in the field have a right to become high-paying consultants as independent contractors;
* Businesses scramble to find the most qualified workers, and compete for those best able to do the job.
* White-collar criminals, in spite of receiving lenient punishment, are generally regarded as poor risks for employment.

These seem like no-brainers. But all of these are signs of a meritocratic society, one where success
and resources are given to those who are seen to contribute the most. Conversely, societies that are not
meritocratic are the opposite, and historically have not been rare. Societies where promotions are doled
out based on connections or nobility, company offices were reserved for people from favored classes
or social elites, arrangements that tied employees to their employers until they were released; these
have all been common arrangements historically.

The reason I bring this up is because for much of the perverse public, Walter White is seen
as the paragon of meritocracy and the sort of attitude our culture should cultivate. People who
talk like this have mistaken merit for overweening pride and ambition. Merit is boring, and
a true meritocracy is one that works for "the good." Walter White As Paragon is not meritocracy as 
most think of it, and a society that muddies this distinction is in peril.

Lately, much of the criticism of "meritocracy," as it is called, is from "the left." The problem
claimed is that meritocracy as a system makes everyone miserable; those unfortunate to be unsuccessful
are told they deserve their failure based on lack of merit, while those who are successful
are doomed to a hamster-wheel
existence of constant work, self-improvement, and "hustle culture."

One historical leftist critic of meritocracy, Immaual Wallerstien,
has noted that meritocracy in the end serves the
capitalistic class. The owners of capital, far from wanting a tooth-in-claw competition from their workers,
instead want a peaceful, hardworking middle and working class which is, most of all, productive.
Law of the jungle may be order of the day for the capitalist masters of the universe, but when it
comes to the cogs that make their businesses move, they want cooperation, industry, even civic
virtue.

Unfortunately, leftist critics of meritocracy seem to be lacking imagination when it comes to the
alternatives to meritocracy that might arise in its absence. They seem to automatically assume
the only existing alternative is a utopian _egalitarianism_.
It is no surprise Wallerstein made this mistake, as he was a straight-up 
marxist: that is, he thought historical processes
guaranteed equality as an inevitable end-state, with each living according to his means, etc.
More conventional left-leaning critics of meritocracy
are hardly marxists, yet stil express confidence that less meritocracy will somehow lead to a more
equal society.  In such a telling, the defenders of metritocracy are seen as conservatives, "the
right". This right eagerly plays into this narrative by claiming that antiracism efforts are somehow
"antimeritocratic."

Historically and in practice, this fact is hardly accurate. 
Meritocracy as an organizing principal for society
is stunningly recent, a cause of reformers and neoliberals of the 19th century. 
Conservatives using "meritocracy" to to attack diversity initiatives and minority outreach
by companies and governments are being disgenuous. Their underlying purpose is not to strengthen
meritocracy, but to shield the priviledging of certain classes.

In spite of Wallerstein's marxist background, he had insight into a number of things,
and one of which was the nature of the appeal of meritocracy to the capitalist. Capitalists
generally want three things, which tend to conflict:

* Non-interference from the state - an obvious desire
* Strong central government - to allow for predictability and open markets for their goods
* Meritocracy - to deliver a productive workforce, one in where workers could be counted to leave "money on the table"

And by meritocracy is meant _actual merit_; that is, hard work, clean competition,
above-board behavior, professional conduct. That is, the actual more qualified, competent
and better person gets the better job and more power. This is not the "meritocracy"
that is derided by some, with its emphasis on Ivy League degrees, professional
pedigrees, and other behavior seen as "opportunity hoarding." That is more rightly
termed credentialism, and the capitalist class seems neutral about that. Instead,
the capitalists want effective workers, such that they produce an economic surplus on their
investments. Actual true merit does that, leaving lots of money on the table,
and that is what capitalists want.

However, that doesn't mean capitalists themselves are meritocrats. There are certain
things capitalists don't care one whit about even though they favor merit.
For example, rule of law - it's important for fairness and consistency, but corruption does
generally not thwart the capitalistic enterprise. Such enterprises are happy to pile in
if they see an opportunity, and corruption is fine if they can partake. This sets them apart
from the meritocratic employees they compete for; meritocratic winners tend
to be concerned with fairness and clean governance, excessively so from the point of view
of capitalists.

Not all capitalists are like this. Some billionares embrace the meritcratic
ethos of hard work and modest consumption, indeed, crediting to it their fortune.
Think of Warren Buffet, who famously lived in
a modest house in a small midwestern city as he was turning millions into billions. But many of
the capitalists regard themselves differently, indeed a class above, their hard-working underlings; perhaps
as warriors in a tooth-and-claw fight with other capitalists, perhaps as aristocrats whose
wealth allow them to rule over their inferiors.

The result is a tension between the values of the capitalist and the values of the meritocratic
class that the capitalist class is so dependent on for their wealth. The educated, meritocratic classes,
and particularly the highly-educated workers that produce the goods with the largest margins,
have their own non-capitalistic set of values, one that values things such as justice,
clean governance, fairness and transparency. This may explain why some
wealthy people take great pains to fight such things, even if may be self-defeating to their
own enterprises; it's the best way to remind the meritocratic class who is actually in charge.
