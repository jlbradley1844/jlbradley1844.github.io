## The Anti-Meritocratic Bubble
_In which a perpetual-motion finance machine is dedicated toward the decimation of the
professional and artistic classes._

As I've probably mentioned, at one time I was an artificial intelligence researcher.
This was in the mid-nineties, right before the predictable coming of The Second AI
Winter. At that time, expert systems and rules-based systems were the way forward,
and researchers like I were looking at things like Bayesian networks to fix the
brittleness of rules-based AI systems. At that time neural networks were hard to
deal with and data for training them was hard to come by. It hardly seemed the sort
of thing to bear practical fruit. Fast forward thirty years, and now I have to
spend my valuable off-hours learning about studies I got a B in because it didn't
seem applicable to my dissertation.

Neural nets power the large language models (LLMs) behind OpenAI, Claude, and
countless other buzzworth AI companies, and boy did they turn out to be successful.
Since my studies, I've never used a rules-based system other than in extremely constrained fashion.
Meanwhile I'm running Claude Code for a few hours every day. Obviously, when it
came to AI, _this_ technology represents the "killer app" that is going to drive
widespread adoption.

As of now, LLMs have proven themselves adept at certain sorts of desktop grunt
work. For most people, they act as an "improved Google" (or more like, Google
before they decided to break it to increase their ad click-thru count). In the
case of software programming, two years' of end-user experience have led to a set of practices
that can notably increase the productivity of programmers. Similar things seem
fated in areas such as law, medicine, editing and a host of white collar jobs.

As such, this technology seems to be a "force multiplier" for accomplished professionals,
freeing them up from the grunt work they'd otherwise hand off to an intern or
have to do themselves. For new employees, it's something of a high-powered
assistant and tutor - a replacement for the busy senior professional they'd otherwise be blocked
by when they're unable to figure something out on their own, ensuring they can
contribute from day one.

And as far as the business opportunity this presents to these AI businesses,
this is _real money_ we're talking about. Claude code has already achieved
massive penetration within the software engineering profession. The basic license,
which suffices for many, is just $20/month, but some engineers, particularly
contractors and startup engineers, have opted for the $200/month license.
At current market penetration rates, we're looking at many billions of dollars of
revenue - a probably moneymaker even with the server farms necessary to power
it all. Extrapolating from Claude code's success, we're looking at an opportunity
in the tens of billions of dollars, even more, with eventual efficiencies in
machine learning leading to tens of billions in profit. This opportunity could eventually
become one of the largest profit centers for "big tech." In exchange for
the profits from delivering this moneymaker, what does society get?
The painfully flat productivity curve for
knowledge workers and office workers finally tilts up, leading to
increased "money on the table" for both profits and paychecks. Indeed,
knowledge workers would become even more essential to their employers
due to their productivty.

**That was never the plan.**

If the AI investment bubble produces tens of millions of dollars in profit,
this represents a _massive failure_ on the part of both the Masters of
the Univers who are dreaming of this AI-driven future and their investors,
who are on the hook for multiple hundreds of _billions_ of dollars. The capital
spend of these companies on AI centers in 2026 is a massive collective
_700 BILLION DOLLARS_. Should AI fail to produce the staggering amonut of
revenue necessary to recoup these investments, the
eventual unwind would lead to staggering losses for big tech as the arcane
web of cross-investment falls apart.

What is worse for some of these investors and their boosters is potentially the loss
of their grand vision. A *superintelligence*
that would invent itself and eventually *replace all professional workers.*
As Peter Thiel said, an end to the "Tax on Capital" paid in the form of
wages by business owners, the only people who count for anything in
his world. And a hoped-for end to the "creative classes," those nasty
high-paid, highly-educated people who didn't get the memo and turn
conservative and reactionary when they got their first six-figure paycheck,
like they were supposed to. As punishment for their overweening
impertinence an arrogance, this superintelligence
would render them all underpaid and useless. Likewise, it would collapse demand
for higher education, another enemy of reactionaries everywhere.

**The plan wasn't to make professionals _more_ valuable. It was to
make them _less_ valuable. Disposable, even.**

And so while Anthropic keeps grabbing headlines for tools which are
actually useful, if perhaps not break-even from a business point of
view, we have dozens of other companies pretending the purpose of AI
is to produce high quality _finished professional product_ that is supposed
to make the skills of prefessionals, artisans and experts redundant.
The result of such assertion has been a backlash against the tsunami of
undistinguished artless output known as "AI slop."

Now, had said companies concentrated on tools for professionals, one
could see the obvious business opportunity. That's how professionals
are using them anyway. Adam Neely's excellent video post on Sumo,
intentionally or not, elides how musicians are using Sumo - just a way
of getting cheap rough-draft songs, not as a way to craft finished
songs. Something of quality in-between their
first simple piano arrangement and an expensive paid demo, which did
not exist before. Presumably the design tools are being used by designers
in a similar fashion. The founders of the company pose this as a way
to "make everyone a creator," but the result for the unskilled is hardly
art - without the professional skill to make finished product, one is
basically listening to personalized muzak. Fun for yourself, but no one
else would want to listen to it.

It turns out a professional with a very productive tool is a very
productive professional. Perhaps you may be able to lower the number
of professionals. But you will not get rid of them. And here's the thing:
professionals don't simply "stay put" in the labor market. They typically
find other opportunities to use their talents. Leaving the professionals
that remain just as "overpaid" and "spoiled" as they always were.

Worse yet, many compnies are constrained not by lack of work, but by
lack of professionals able to do the work. Should one company lay off
the "spoiled, underpaid takers" in the professional class, another will scoop
them back up to win more  business for themselves.

So the AI revolution is not likely to adjust the class dynamics in the
favor of those who view the wages they pay as an unjust "tax of labor."
If engineering wages and other professional wages will go up, it will be
in spite of the efforts of the AI titans who were accidentally responsible
for the professionals' improved productivity.

The last few years have proved a real education to the people of the
world on the personal characters of the titans that control both big
tech and finance. Many of them posed for years as being on the side of
the educated classes, only to show in the past year-and-a-half that
no, they were only joking, they actually have no trouble with fascism
and little patience with democracy, and what really motivates them to
deploy hundreds of billions of capital is the vision of a world where
the 99.9% of the masses of humanity is converted into an undifferentiated,
fungible "pool of labor,"
who should be nothing but grateful for the opportunity to work.

How else to explain the sudden charms of Universal Basic Income among
people who have ordinarily fought against any social spending
whatsoever? Universal Basic
Income is sold as a great leveler between the newly unemployed and the
employed - see, nobody's starving anymore. But being on this level of
welfare doesn't make someone
independent, it makes them _dependent_. And the people proposing that
certainly know that difference. The time between the implementation of
Universal Basic Income and the overwhelming calls to revoke the vote of
anyone on such dependence is likely to be measure in milliseconds.

So when the web of AI finance collapses late this year or next year, it's
likely few of us will be enjoying the fireworks, any more than we enjoyed
2008. The only silver lining will be the death of the unspoken dreams
of neofeudalism that powered it all.
